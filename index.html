Research vision

Our clearest molecules-to-behavior accounts of learning come from simple paradigms like the sea slug’s gill-withdrawal reflex. Yet we lack a comparable mechanistic account of the skills that most define us: speech, music, and sport—complex learned skills profoundly disrupted by aging and neurodegenerative disease. Birdsong offers a unique bridge. Behaviorally, it resembles speech and music: a culturally transmitted motor sequence learned through imitation and prolonged practice. Experimentally, it provides the anatomical foothold that made simple models so powerful: a dedicated neural circuit that constrains where to look for the mechanisms of learning.

Realizing this potential has long been hampered by difficulties in quantifying learning and adapting tools to measure and manipulate the involved circuitry. I overcame these barriers by combining AI-based quantification with optogenetic, chemogenetic, and biosensor tools adapted to the songbird, gaining direct access to the synapses and signaling pathways that drive learning.

In collaborative work we showed that dopamine in the song basal ganglia evaluates the quality of individual renditions and is required for learning—the first demonstration that dopamine instructs a natural, internally evaluated skill (Qi et al., 2025, Nature). I then used AI analysis across 15 million syllable renditions with pathway-specific manipulations to identify the specific cortico-basal ganglia synapses where learning first occurs (Schreiner et al., Nature).

These discoveries reveal the songbird’s power: the computational ingredients of reinforcement learning can now be assigned to defined molecules and synapses. With these technical and conceptual advances, my lab will pursue three linked questions:

1. How are transient patterns of synaptic activity converted into motor change?
2. How are those changes stabilized into skills that persist for a lifetime?
3. How do biological constraints optimize learning?

Together, these questions define a research program that moves from the first moment of plasticity at a synapse to the computational logic of a learning system, with long-term relevance for understanding learned vocal behavior in health, aging, and disease.
