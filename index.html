Research vision

Our clearest molecules-to-behavior accounts of learning come from simple paradigms like the sea slug’s gill-withdrawal reflex. Yet we lack a comparable mechanistic account of the skills that most define us: speech, music, and sport. Birdsong offers a unique bridge. Behaviorally, it resembles speech and music: a culturally transmitted motor sequence learned through imitation and prolonged practice. Experimentally, it provides the anatomical foothold that made simple models so powerful: a dedicated neural circuit that constrains where to look for the mechanisms of learning.

Realizing this potential has long been hampered by difficulties in quantifying learning and adapting tools to measure and manipulate the involved circuitry. I overcame these barriers by combining AI-based quantification of millions of renditions with optogenetic, chemogenetic, and biosensor tools adapted to the songbird, gaining direct access to the synapses and signaling pathways that drive learning.

We showed that dopamine in a song-specialized basal ganglia region evaluates the quality of individual songs and is required for learning—the first demonstration that dopamine instructs a natural, internally evaluated skill (Qi et al., 2025, Nature). I then isolated the specific cortico-basal ganglia synapses where song learning first occurs, a locus predicted by an influential model fifteen years earlier, but untested until now (Schreiner et al., 2026, Nature). In the same study, augmenting activity in this circuit accelerated learning at a cost to copying fidelity, indicating that the system does not simply maximize the rate of learning but instead tunes it.

These studies establish the technical and conceptual foundation for a true molecules-to-behavior account of complex skill learning. My independent program addresses three linked questions:

  1. Acquisition: How do molecular signals at defined synapses drive motor change?
  2. Consolidation: How are newly acquired changes stabilized into lasting skills?
  3. Optimization: How do biological constraints shape learning?

My program will bring the mechanistic depth of classic learning models to a culturally transmitted skill that resembles speech and music, with relevance to health, aging, and disease.
