Research vision

Our clearest molecules-to-behavior accounts of learning come from simple paradigms like the sea slug’s gill-withdrawal reflex. Yet we lack a comparable mechanistic account of the skills that most define us: speech, music, and sport. Birdsong offers a unique bridge. Behaviorally, it resembles speech and music: a culturally transmitted motor sequence learned through imitation and prolonged practice. Experimentally, it provides the anatomical foothold that made simple models so powerful: a dedicated neural circuit that constrains where to look for the mechanisms of learning.

Realizing this potential has long been hampered by difficulties in quantifying learning and adapting tools to measure and manipulate the involved circuitry. I overcame these barriers by combining AI-based quantification of millions of renditions with optogenetic, chemogenetic, and biosensor tools adapted to the songbird, gaining direct access to the synapses and signaling pathways that drive learning.

In collaborative work, we showed that dopamine in the song basal ganglia evaluates vocal performance and instructs learning (Qi et al., 2025, Nature). I then localized learning to specific cortico-basal ganglia synapses and showed that increasing activity in this circuit accelerates learning at a cost to tutor-copying fidelity (Schreiner et al., 2026, Nature). These discoveries provide a synaptic foothold for understanding how a complex, culturally transmitted skill is learned.

Now that I know where learning occurs, I can ask how. My independent lab will use this foothold to build a molecules-to-behavior account of complex skill learning through three linked questions:

  1. Acquisition:How does delayed evaluation modify the correct synapses?
  2. Consolidation: Which molecular changes stabilize learning into lasting skill?
  3. Optimization:What sets the speed and fidelity of learning?

My program will bring molecular and synaptic resolution to a culturally transmitted skill, revealing how experience is transformed into behavior that can be passed from one individual to the next.
